Four decisions I make on every project.

Not a portfolio. A look at the reasoning underneath one.

01
What gets fixed first

Not everything imperfect is costing you customers.

There's always more that could be improved than time allows. I start with what's actually losing you business today — a confusing homepage, a broken contact form, a page that takes eight seconds to load — before I touch anything that's merely imperfect. Polish comes after the bleeding stops.

02
When simple beats impressive

A hand-built animation only earns its place if it helps someone understand something faster.

Otherwise it's decoration slowing down your page load. I've talked clients out of effects they were excited about because the honest answer was that it would cost them speed and clarity for very little in return. The best decision is sometimes the invisible one.

03
What “done” actually means

I don't call a site finished because it looks good. I call it finished when a stranger gets it in five seconds.

Looking good is necessary, not sufficient. Before anything ships, I ask one question: could someone who's never heard of this business land on the homepage and know what it does, who it's for, and what to do next — in five seconds? If not, it's not done, no matter how polished it looks.

04
When the honest answer costs me the sale

Sometimes what a business needs isn't a redesign. It's three things fixed.

I'll say that on a call even when a full rebuild would pay more — because a site that actually gets fixed serves both of us better than one that got oversold.

Hettie Orange Designs · Design, publishing & web · Atlanta, GA

Her name was misspelled in her own browser tab.

The situation: Hettie had two decades of design, publishing, and print work behind her — magazines, newsletters, books, and annual reports for churches, nonprofits, and authors — and a website that showed almost none of it. The browser tab read “Home page | Hetti Orange”: the content system's default title, with her name misspelled. The menu still listed the template's demo pages. One of the featured blocks on her own homepage described an outdoor space with “vivid colors and abstract patterns” — filler that shipped with the theme and never got taken out. None of that was her doing. She'd been sold a heavy content system and left to finish configuring it herself.

What I fixed first: the things quietly costing her the most. A title that spells her name correctly. Pages that describe her work instead of a theme demo's. Then the spine — home, services, work, plans, about, contact — each answering a question a prospective client actually asks. Print and web sit side by side, because that combination is what makes her unusual. And the voice changed from “We are your creative force” to “I'm Hettie Orange,” because the second one is true and the first one is what a template told her to say.

The honest call: she didn't need another content system. A one-person studio shouldn't be patching a CMS to keep a six-page site online, so I rebuilt it as plain HTML and CSS — nothing to update, nothing to break, nothing to log into. The portfolio page was built to start small and grow rather than padded to look busy on day one, and the monthly care plans got a page of their own, because an ongoing relationship is worth explaining properly instead of burying in a footer.
